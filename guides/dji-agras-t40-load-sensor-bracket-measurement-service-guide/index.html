DJI Agras T40 Load Sensor Brackets: Measurement Integrity and Service Acceptance

Published 2026-09-28 by UNITED UAV Official

A load-sensor bracket is easy to underestimate because it is neither the electronic sensing element nor the application software that displays payload. Yet a weighing system can report useful data only when the applied load reaches the sensor through the intended mechanical path. A bent bracket, constrained roller, displaced shaft, contaminated contact, loose interface or unintended secondary support can create zero offset, hysteresis, side sensitivity or poor repeatability even when the sensor and wiring remain electrically functional. This guide uses the five material-numbered variants in the UNITED UAV DJI Agras T40 load-sensor bracket listing to build a practical engineering workflow for inspection, fault isolation, procurement and post-service acceptance. It does not substitute for DJI service documentation, prescribe undocumented dimensions or authorize structural repair. Its purpose is to help operators and qualified technicians separate mechanical evidence from calibration and electronic evidence before a part is ordered or the aircraft is returned to agricultural operations.

1. Understand what the bracket can and cannot explain

DJI describes the Agras T40 as supporting a 40 kg spraying payload and a 50 kg spreading payload at sea level, subject to environmental conditions and the payload recommended by the DJI Agras app. The aircraft also uses weight sensing to monitor remaining payload. These system-level facts make weighing integrity operationally relevant, but they do not mean every incorrect payload display originates in a bracket. The complete measurement chain includes the loaded tank or operation box, mechanical supports, contact interfaces, weight-sensor hardware, cabling, signal processing, configuration, tare state and application display. Diagnosis must keep those layers separate.

The store listing contains brackets, rollers and roller shafts; it does not list the electronic weight-sensor module on this page. The reasonable engineering role of these parts is therefore mechanical support and load transfer within the assembly, not direct electrical measurement. A damaged mechanical interface can disturb the force delivered to a sound sensor, while a failed sensor or cable can produce a bad reading around perfectly aligned brackets. Treat a bracket fault as a hypothesis supported by geometry, contact and repeatability evidence. Do not replace visible hardware merely because the displayed weight is wrong, and do not recalibrate repeatedly to hide a mechanically unstable zero.

2. Preserve the five catalog identities and diagram quantities

The UNITED UAV listing identifies YC.JG.ZS002397 as Load Sensor Bracket Roller, with four shown in the diagram; YC.JG.ZS002224 as Load Sensor Bracket (Front), quantity one; YC.WJ.ZZ002618 as Spray Tank Fixing Piece Roller Shaft, quantity four; YC.JG.ZS002226 as Load Sensor Bracket (Right), quantity one; and YC.JG.ZS002225 as Load Sensor Bracket (Left), quantity one. Front, right and left are distinct material-numbered parts. Their directional names are not interchangeable descriptions, and the diagram quantities should be recorded rather than assumed from the number of variants in an online selector.

Keep the exact material number separate from the storefront SKU. The roller-shaft SKU may contain a suffix even though the displayed material number remains YC.WJ.ZZ002618. A purchase request should therefore include diagram item number, material number, material name, diagram quantity, required replacement quantity, aircraft model, privately communicated serial identifier and photographs of the installed position. Diagram quantity describes the illustrated assembly, not an automatic instruction to buy that many. A technician may find that one damaged roller requires replacement while the remaining interfaces only require documented inspection, or may conclude that paired wear requires a broader corrective action.

3. Model the weighing system as a load path

A useful mental model begins with the total applied payload and asks how that force reaches each sensing point. In an ideal path, supports seat consistently, movable interfaces move as intended, and no hose, latch, debris bridge or distorted panel carries an unintended portion of the load. In a non-ideal path, friction or hard contact can bypass part of the sensor response. The display may then vary with loading direction, tank seating or vibration. This behavior is commonly described using zero offset, sensitivity error, non-linearity and hysteresis, but those terms are measurement categories rather than proof of a specific failed component.

The bracket rollers and shafts deserve attention because a rolling or pivoting interface is expected to accommodate a defined relative movement or seating action. If an interface binds, the measured result can depend on whether load was added or removed. If a shaft is displaced, the load distribution may become asymmetric. If a bracket is bent, the tank or attachment may contact another structure. These are engineering possibilities to test, not undocumented claims about the exact internal geometry of the T40. Use the current parts diagram and DJI service information to identify the actual contact surfaces before drawing a causal conclusion.

4. Convert a weighing complaint into reproducible evidence

Replace descriptions such as inaccurate or unstable with a test record. Capture the aircraft identifier, installed spray or spread system, firmware and app versions, surface levelness, tank state, visible residue, displayed value before tare, reference load or known water volume, loading sequence, settling time, repeated displayed values and any warnings. Photograph the aircraft from the front and both sides without obscuring the support interfaces. Record whether the error appeared after a collision, transport event, tank removal, cleaning, parts replacement, firmware update or long storage. Keep chemical handling outside a diagnostic test whenever clean water or approved inert reference loads can provide the required evidence.

Repeatability matters more than a single favorable reading. Apply the same approved reference condition several times, fully unload between runs where the procedure requires it, and record every result rather than only the closest value. A stable offset that disappears after correct tare differs from a zero that wanders after each load cycle. A proportional error differs from an abrupt step at one load. A reading that changes when the tank is reseated points toward a different branch than one that remains wrong across consistent mechanical setups. The test should reveal a pattern that the responsible technician can reproduce.

5. Use zero, span, linearity and hysteresis as separate checks

Zero is the reading with the applicable tank or operation box empty and the aircraft prepared exactly as the DJI calibration procedure specifies. Span is the change in indicated value for a known added load. Linearity asks whether intermediate points follow the expected relationship instead of being correct only near one calibration point. Hysteresis compares a point reached while adding load with the same point reached while removing load. These four checks answer different questions. Passing tare alone does not prove correct span, and passing one loaded point does not prove repeatability or freedom from mechanical binding.

For a controlled multi-point record, define error at each point as indicated load minus reference load, and relative error as that difference divided by the nonzero reference load. Define hysteresis at a point as the absolute difference between the increasing-load and decreasing-load readings. Do not invent acceptance limits from these formulas. The limits must come from current DJI documentation or the qualified service organization's approved standard. A known volume of clean water can provide an approximate reference because water mass is related to volume, but temperature, container accuracy, residual liquid and displayed units must be accounted for before it is treated as traceable calibration evidence.

6. Inspect the aircraft before changing calibration

Calibration mathematically aligns a measurement system; it cannot straighten a bracket, release a seized roller or remove a secondary contact path. Before tare or weight calibration, power down and remove the flight battery as required for mechanical inspection. Under the applicable service procedure, inspect the front, left and right bracket locations for deformation, cracks, displaced interfaces, polished rub marks, corrosion, residue buildup and unequal gaps. Check the rollers and shafts for correct seating and evidence of flat spotting, binding or migration. Do not force a roller to move or use an improvised lubricant that could change friction, retain abrasive material or attack surrounding components.

Also inspect everything that can influence tank seating without being sold on this page: attachment points, adjacent structure, hoses, wiring clearance, latches and any object trapped between the payload assembly and airframe. A hose placed under tension may behave like an unintended spring; a residue bridge can become a hard support; a cable can restrict movement. Record these as observed conditions rather than asserting that they alter the reading until a controlled before-and-after test demonstrates the effect. If a structural crack or impact damage extends beyond the bracket assembly, stop the local investigation and escalate the aircraft for broader qualified inspection.

7. Distinguish front, left and right asymmetry

YC.JG.ZS002224, YC.JG.ZS002226 and YC.JG.ZS002225 identify the front, right and left brackets respectively. Directional identification should survive every stage of the job: initial photograph, removed-part tag, replacement package, installation record and acceptance image. A mirrored-looking part is not verified interchangeable. If the complaint changes with tank orientation or a controlled load position, map the behavior to the corresponding support region, but do not assume that the nearest bracket is the cause. The sensor arrangement and structural load sharing must be established from DJI service data.

Asymmetry analysis is most useful as a comparison. Document left and right clearances with the same camera position or measurement method, check whether corresponding rollers seat in the same way, and note whether witness marks occur on only one side. If an approved test permits reference loads at defined positions, repeat them consistently and observe whether the indicated total depends on position. Position sensitivity can indicate a non-uniform load path, but it can also reflect setup error. The conclusion should state what changed, what stayed constant and what the technician inspected—not simply that one side looked different.

8. Separate mechanical faults from calibration and electronics

Use three diagnostic branches. The mechanical branch covers brackets, rollers, shafts, tank seating and unintended contact. The calibration branch covers incorrect tare state, an unsuitable calibration condition or an invalid calibration constant. The electrical and processing branch covers the weight-sensor module, cabling, distribution hardware, spraying electronics, firmware and app interpretation. Evidence may cross branches, but the corrective action should identify which branch was proven. A repeatable physical bind should not be labeled firmware; a clean mechanical inspection does not by itself prove that the K value or sensor signal is correct.

DJI's published troubleshooting for T-series spraying faults directs technicians working with a weight-sensor issue to reinstall the module, rewrite the K value, perform tare calibration with an empty spray tank, and then consider module, cable-distribution-board and spraying-board replacement. That sequence shows why bracket replacement should not become a universal response to an abnormal value. It also shows why undocumented parameter changes are inappropriate. K-value work and electronic replacement belong to the authorized procedure, with original values and results recorded. If the symptom appeared after a software update, capture the versions and alerts before altering hardware.

9. Control tare and weight calibration conditions

The DJI Agras app exposes weight-sensor settings. DJI's T40 maintenance guidance calls for tare calibration with the tank empty, followed by a known-water check and weight calibration when indicated. The aircraft must be on the surface and in the configuration specified by the current instructions. A sloped pad, residual liquid, attached tools, hose tension, wind loading or contact from nearby equipment can corrupt the reference condition. Write down the exact empty configuration so that a future operator can reproduce it; empty should not mean visually almost empty.

Do not use calibration as an iterative adjustment until the number looks plausible. One properly controlled tare and one documented weight calibration are more informative than many undocumented attempts. Record the pre-calibration zero, reference quantity, post-calibration result and app confirmation. If calibration fails, will not hold, or produces different answers after reseating the tank, return to fault isolation. DJI has previously issued firmware changes addressing weight-sensor detection stability, so firmware state belongs in the record, but a software history does not excuse a damaged mechanical support. Both must be checked on their own evidence.

10. Decide whether a roller, shaft or bracket is the corrective item

A roller candidate should have evidence at the roller: binding, damage, abnormal wear, contamination that cannot be resolved under the approved cleaning method, or failure to seat as documented. A shaft candidate should have evidence involving retention, alignment, wear or damage at YC.WJ.ZZ002618. A directional bracket candidate should have confirmed deformation, fracture, damaged mounting geometry or an interface condition that remains after adjacent causes are excluded. Visible proximity is not sufficient. Replacing the cheapest item without proving the load-path defect can leave the measurement error unchanged and disturb otherwise serviceable interfaces.

Replacement scope should include collateral inspection. If a shaft migrated, determine why and inspect its roller and bracket. If a roller seized, inspect the shaft and contact path. If a bracket bent after impact, inspect the attachment structure and relevant sensor interface rather than treating the bracket as an isolated sacrificial part. Conversely, do not replace all five variants merely because the listing groups them together. The final service record should explain why each renewed part was necessary and how the acceptance results demonstrate closure of the original complaint.

11. Build an acceptance test that exposes hidden friction

A robust acceptance sequence has gates. Gate 1 confirms correct material numbers, directional orientation, diagram quantities where applicable, roller and shaft seating, clearances and a clean empty condition. Gate 2 records unloaded zero after the required settling period. Gate 3 applies at least one authorized known-load point. Gate 4 repeats that point after complete unloading. Gate 5, where the approved procedure permits, uses several increasing and decreasing points to expose non-linearity and hysteresis. Gate 6 reseats the payload assembly and repeats a reference point to check configuration repeatability. A failure at any gate returns the aircraft to diagnosis.

The DJI preseason guide offers a practical screening check: after empty-tank tare, add 20 L of water and examine whether the displayed payload falls within the stated broad range before proceeding to weight calibration. Treat that as the manufacturer's screening workflow, not a metrology certificate or a substitute for the current app instructions. For service acceptance, preserve the actual displayed result, water measurement method and test conditions. Do not report only pass. A later trend can be evaluated only when the underlying values are available.

12. Connect weighing integrity to payload safety and application control

The T40 payload limits are not invitations to load the aircraft to a fixed number in every environment. DJI states that payload capability is affected by altitude and temperature and that the Agras app recommends a limit for the current conditions. The maximum should not be exceeded. A weighing error can undermine refill planning and remaining-payload awareness; it can also mislead an operator assessing whether the current load matches the recommended value. The correct response is to repair and verify the measurement system, not to add a guessed correction factor during operations.

For spraying, distinguish weight information from liquid-flow measurement. The weight sensor helps represent payload, while the flow meter reports delivery rate. Agreement between expected mass loss and integrated flow can be a useful cross-check over a controlled interval, but the two measurements have different errors and dynamics. A mismatch is evidence for investigation, not automatic proof that the bracket is wrong. Check for residual liquid, timing differences, density, leakage, flow calibration and sensor behavior. The same discipline applies to spreading: displayed remaining payload and actual material discharge should be reconciled without assuming that one sensor validates the other.

13. Make inspection intervals condition-aware

DJI's T40/T20P manual lists the weight sensor for inspection every 100 operating hours or one month and recommends replacement after 1,000 hours. Consult the current manual and regional service instructions before acting because revisions may change. The interval is a baseline, not a reason to defer inspection after an impact, abnormal reading, repeated tank removal, severe contamination or evidence of support wear. Record both aircraft hours and calendar date. When a component is replaced, record its own time in service so the fleet does not confuse component age with total airframe age.

Condition monitoring can be simple: retain unloaded zero, one standardized reference reading, hysteresis or repeatability result, and photographs of the support interfaces at each scheduled inspection. Plotting those values over time can reveal gradual drift before it becomes an operational complaint. Use the same surface, configuration, reference method and settling time so the trend reflects the aircraft rather than test variation. A sudden step after transport or maintenance deserves immediate investigation. A slow trend still requires action when it reaches the approved limit; trending is not permission to operate indefinitely outside specification.

14. Procure and document the repair as configuration control

Before ordering, give the supplier or service provider the exact T40 model, privately communicated aircraft serial identifier, installed payload system, affected location, diagram item, material number, required quantity if established, original-part photographs and observed failure mode. Ask for written confirmation of fitment and any superseded number. Do not infer that a part fits because its photograph is similar or because T40 and T20P share some other components. The live product page governs current variant availability, while the current DJI parts and service information governs applicability and installation.

Close the work with a traceable matrix: complaint identifier, pre-service zero and load results, mechanical findings, calibration findings, electronic findings, replaced material numbers, discarded-part disposition, post-service values, technician, date and remaining limitations. Attach before-and-after photographs that preserve front, left and right orientation. If fitment, calibration authority or structural condition cannot be verified, stop and escalate to DJI Support or a qualified service channel. The professional standard is not merely an app display that returns to zero; it is a stable, repeatable and documented load path whose output remains valid across the approved operating configuration.
